## Original long-form script draft, target 8–10 minutes after performance edit

[00:00–00:30, cold open. Visual: supplier quotation beside an empty evidence column.]
A vendor says its AI agent will save your team twenty hours a week. You receive a polished demo and a price. Should you sign? Not yet. A demo shows what happened once, under conditions the vendor selected. A procurement decision needs proof of repeatable outcomes, a defined owner, and a cost you understand. Today we will put an AI agent through seven evidence tests. At the end, you will have a scorecard to use before approving a pilot.

[00:30–01:15, establish decision and scope. Visual: purchase funnel.]
First define the job. We will use a fictional procurement-inbox agent that reads supplier emails, extracts quote details, and proposes a comparison table. It does not approve suppliers or send purchase orders. Humans make those decisions. This matters because a tool that drafts a comparison has a different risk profile from a tool that can commit company funds. The buying question is not, “How intelligent is the agent?” It is, “What outcome will we pay for, under what controls?”

[01:15–02:05, test 1: repeatable task evidence. Visual: 50-case test set.]
Test one: repeatability. Ask for a live evaluation on fifty representative cases, including messy PDFs, duplicate suppliers, missing prices, currency conversions and incomplete emails. Select the cases yourself. Freeze the acceptance standard before testing. Measure how many comparisons are correct without material correction, how many require review, and how many fail. If the vendor only shows three perfect examples, record the result as unproven, not as zero defects.

[02:05–02:55, test 2: all-in economics. Visual: cost equation.]
Test two: full workflow economics. Model fees are one line. Include setup, integration, security review, training, human review, rework, exception handling and ongoing support. Here is a fictional illustration. Suppose a team processes four hundred supplier comparisons a month. Manual work takes fifteen minutes per case at a loaded labor rate of forty dollars per hour. The baseline labor value is four thousand dollars a month. If the agent reduces review to six minutes per case, the remaining labor value is sixteen hundred dollars. The gross capacity released is twenty-four hundred dollars. Now assume six hundred dollars in software and processing, three hundred dollars in monthly support, and two hundred dollars in rework. The modeled net capacity-equivalent benefit is thirteen hundred dollars per month, before amortizing setup and other costs. This is NOT thirteen hundred dollars in cash savings unless payroll expenses fall or released time produces measurable additional contribution. Do not present this fictional scenario as vendor performance.

[02:55–03:45, test 3: control of access. Visual: read, draft, approve permissions.]
Test three: access. Which inboxes, files and systems can the agent read? Can it write to supplier records? Can it approve a purchase? Each capability needs an explicit owner and an approval boundary. For the first pilot, read-only extraction and draft-only output should be the default. Ask whether credentials are scoped, short-lived, individually attributable and revocable. An administrator should demonstrate disabling the agent and preventing a further action. If the vendor cannot explain the permission boundary, stop the purchase review.

[03:45–04:35, test 4: evidence trail. Visual: audit record.]
Test four: traceability. Pick one recommendation and follow the evidence. Which document supplied the price? Which exchange rate and date were used? Which fields were uncertain? Who approved the final comparison? The agent should expose enough information for a reviewer to reproduce the conclusion. An impressive answer with no reliable source path is a weak business control. For regulated work, records retention and access rules need specialist review.

[04:35–05:25, test 5: failures and escalation. Visual: three exception cards.]
Test five: failure handling. Deliberately introduce an altered invoice, a missing tax field and a conflicting supplier name. Does the system flag uncertainty, or does it fill gaps confidently? Where do exceptions go? How quickly does a human take ownership? Ask for documented rollback and incident-response procedures. A pilot should define a maximum acceptable critical-error rate and suspend autonomous actions if the limit is breached. The specific limit depends on business risk, not a universal percentage.

[05:25–06:15, test 6: independent evaluation. Visual: vendor demo versus buyer test.]
Test six: independent evaluation. Separate the supplier's marketing metrics from the buyer's acceptance test. Ask for a locked sample, independent human review, and before-and-after results on the same workload. Measure median time, 90th-percentile time, accepted outputs, exceptions, rework and total cost per accepted result. NIST's voluntary framework is a useful organizing reference: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. It does not certify any specific product by itself.

[06:15–07:05, test 7: exit and ownership. Visual: export, disable, migrate.]
Test seven: exit. If the supplier raises prices, changes a model, or stops supporting an integration, can your company retrieve its records, revoke credentials and move the workflow? Confirm data export format, deletion terms, logs, service levels, support response, incident notification and ownership of prompts or configurations where relevant. Procurement should be able to end the relationship without losing essential business records.

[07:05–08:15, scorecard. Visual: seven rows, pass/evidence missing/fail.]
Now make the decision. Each test gets one of three outcomes: PASS with evidence, OPEN because proof is missing, or FAIL because a mandatory control did not work. Do not average a failed security boundary against a strong demo score. Any critical failure blocks production approval. An open item creates a limited pilot condition and a named owner, not an automatic yes. A pass means the buyer has inspected the evidence for the agreed scope, not that the product is risk-free.

Here is a practical procurement gate. First, define the exact workflow and the human decision owner. Second, run a bounded test using buyer-selected cases. Third, compare accepted outputs and all-in economics against a baseline. Fourth, test permissions, audit logs and failure recovery. Fifth, sign a pilot agreement with measurable exit criteria. Only then consider expanding access. Each stage should reduce uncertainty rather than increase commitment before evidence arrives.

[08:15–08:55, news context and caveat. Visual: IBM announcement date and availability tags.]
Why is this urgent now? On October twenty-seventh, IBM announced additional identity, access and evaluation controls for enterprise agents. Its release distinguishes generally available products from technical previews. This is evidence of the direction of the market, not independent proof that every promised control works in every customer environment. Ask every vendor to identify what is available today, what is in preview, and what has been demonstrated on a workload like yours.

[08:55–09:25, close. Visual: scorecard and series promise.]
Before buying an AI agent, ask for evidence on seven things: repeatability, economics, access, traceability, failures, evaluation and exit. If a vendor cannot supply the evidence, you have learned something valuable before spending the budget. PrismBay AI helps founders, operators and executives implement practical AI business systems. In the next episode, we will show how to calculate cost per accepted result using a simple worksheet.
