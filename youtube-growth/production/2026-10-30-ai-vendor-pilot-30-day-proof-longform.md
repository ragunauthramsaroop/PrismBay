## Original 9-11 minute narration script (target 1,330-1,480 spoken words)

### 00:00 | Cold open: the invoice is not the outcome
An AI supplier says their system will save your team twenty hours every week. Their demonstration looks convincing. Their monthly price is affordable. But what exactly are you buying? A tool that produces answers, or a process that produces accepted work?

Here is the test I would run before signing an annual agreement. Give the supplier thirty days, a narrow workflow, a fixed spending ceiling, and a scorecard the supplier does not control. At the end, make one of three decisions: proceed, redesign, or stop.

I will show you the scorecard, the numbers, the approval gates, and the decision memo. Every number in the example is fictional. The method is intended for adaptation, not a promise of savings.

### 00:55 | Choose one workflow, not an entire department
Start with a task your team already performs repeatedly. Imagine a procurement analyst preparing comparisons from approved supplier quotations. Today, a person reads the documents, extracts the commercial terms, flags missing evidence, and prepares a comparison for a manager. The manager still approves the decision.

That is a testable workflow. 'Automate procurement' is not. In a thirty-day pilot, keep the input format, output format, review standard, and approval owner stable. Exclude purchase orders, bank-detail changes, external communications and payments from the agent's authority. A successful comparison is not permission to place an order.

Write the acceptance standard before the first trial: required fields complete, numerical totals correct, source references present, confidential information handled according to policy, and human reviewer approval recorded. If a result fails one mandatory condition, count it as rejected even when the prose looks polished.

### 01:55 | Establish the baseline before the vendor touches the task
Take a sample of recent work. Measure how long the existing process takes, the number of accepted results, rework, error rates and any outside software costs. Record the distribution, not only the average. An unusually easy week should not determine an annual contract.

For the illustration, assume the human process takes eight minutes per accepted comparison, with a loaded labor rate of twenty-five dollars per hour. That is three dollars and thirty-three cents per accepted result, excluding any other costs. This is a hypothetical rate, not a market benchmark.

The vendor must receive the same task definition and evaluation rubric. Otherwise, a vendor demonstration using ideal data is being compared against a real process handling messy documents. That is not a fair test.

### 02:50 | Four gates before the pilot starts
Gate one is data permission. Identify which information enters the system, where it is stored, who has access, how long it is retained, and what happens on termination. Do not upload sensitive live records merely to find out whether a product works.

Gate two is action authority. The pilot should be read-only where possible. Require separate human approval for purchasing, publishing, deleting or changing financial details. Test whether permissions are enforced by the actual tools, not only described in a prompt.

Gate three is evidence. Require an audit record for each run: input identifier, model or workflow version, tool calls, reviewer, acceptance decision, exceptions and charges. An attractive dashboard is not a substitute for a traceable result.

Gate four is exit. Confirm how to export your records, revoke access, delete data according to the agreement, and stop billing. If the supplier cannot answer these four questions clearly, the correct pilot decision is not 'go live and hope.'

These are PrismBay's proposed procurement gates, not a certification issued by NIST. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and Playbook offer voluntary guidance on governance, third-party risks, measurement and management. Adapt those principles to your own obligations and risk tolerance.

### 04:05 | The 30-day plan
Days one through five: agree on the scope, acceptance rubric, permissions, price ceiling, and baseline. Choose an owner from operations and an independent reviewer. Agree on which tasks are too sensitive for the pilot.

Days six through twelve: run a small supervised sample. Review every result. Record failed extractions, wrong numbers, invented references, missing fields, slow responses and human interventions. Fix the workflow once, with a version note, instead of quietly changing the test each day.

Days thirteen through twenty-three: expand to the agreed sample size without changing the scoring rules. Capture actual vendor charges, human review minutes, exceptions and accepted outputs. Apply a hard spending stop and a maximum retry count.

Days twenty-four through thirty: repeat a holdout sample the supplier has not optimized against. Reconcile charges and export the audit records. Prepare the decision memo. The vendor gets a chance to explain failures, but not to erase them from the denominator.

### 05:10 | Work the math, including the work AI did not finish
Suppose the pilot attempts four hundred comparisons. Three hundred and twenty meet every acceptance criterion. Eighty fail. The acceptance rate is eighty percent.

Assume the vendor charges one hundred and eighty dollars. Every attempted comparison takes two minutes of human review. At twenty-five dollars an hour, that review costs about three hundred and thirty-three dollars. Handling the eighty failed cases takes another five minutes each, costing about one hundred and sixty-seven dollars. Allocate one hundred and fifty dollars of implementation cost to the pilot period.

The total is eight hundred and thirty dollars, rounded. Divide by three hundred and twenty accepted comparisons, not four hundred attempts. The cost is about two dollars and fifty-nine cents per accepted result.

Now compare equivalent outputs. Three hundred and twenty human-produced accepted comparisons, at eight minutes and twenty-five dollars an hour, cost about one thousand and sixty-seven dollars. The illustrative difference is about two hundred and thirty-seven dollars, or twenty-two percent of the human baseline.

Do not call this a verified saving. The example assumes comparable quality, comparable inputs, the stated labor rates, and an honest allocation of implementation work. It also excludes any unmeasured transition or supervision burden. Your real calculation needs your own records.

And here is the twist. Imagine your mandatory acceptance threshold was ninety percent. The pilot reached eighty percent. Even with the apparent unit-cost advantage, the pilot fails its quality gate. The correct decision is revise or stop, not sign an annual contract because the spreadsheet looks attractive.

### 06:45 | Measure five signals, not one
The first signal is acceptance rate: accepted outputs divided by attempted outputs. The second is cost per accepted result: vendor charges plus review, rework and allocated implementation, divided by accepted outputs.

Third is exception severity. One incorrect supplier bank account is not equivalent to a formatting typo. Use predefined severity levels and stop the pilot when a critical failure appears.

Fourth is time to accepted result. Include queue time and human corrections. Faster model output does not automatically mean faster business completion.

Fifth is audit completeness. For every accepted decision, can an independent reviewer reconstruct the source, actions, approvals and cost? If the answer is no, the pilot has not demonstrated operational control.

### 07:40 | Vendor promises and evidence
Ask suppliers to distinguish measured customer outcomes from controlled demonstrations, simulations and marketing estimates. Require the test conditions and the denominator behind any percentage.

This is not theoretical caution. In 2025 the US Federal Trade Commission finalized an order against an AI-content-detection supplier over unsupported accuracy claims. In a separate 2026 announcement, the FTC described a proposed settlement involving alleged deceptive AI-related business-opportunity earnings claims. Those are different matters, and neither proves all AI suppliers are unreliable. They show why buyers should request substantiation instead of accepting a headline number.

The lesson is simple: a vendor claim is a hypothesis until your own acceptance test produces evidence.

### 08:30 | The one-page decision memo
Put the decision on one page. State the workflow, owner, test dates, sample size, accepted outputs, critical incidents, actual charges, review hours, cost per accepted result, baseline comparison, data handling, and any unresolved obligations.

Choose GO only when every mandatory gate passes and the measured value survives review. Choose REVISE when the use case has promise but a correctable problem blocks acceptance. Choose STOP when the supplier cannot protect the data, enforce authority, produce an audit trail, or deliver acceptable results.

Never convert a failed pilot into a successful pilot by changing the success threshold afterward. Preserve the failed result and describe the change as a new experiment.

### 09:15 | Closing
A thirty-day pilot does not answer every question about a long-term AI system. It does answer a useful one: does this supplier deliver accepted work, under your controls, at a cost you understand?
