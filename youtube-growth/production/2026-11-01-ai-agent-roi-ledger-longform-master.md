## Full original narration script
### 00:00 to 00:48 | The promise versus the ledger
A company processes six thousand invoices every month. A vendor offers an AI agent and promises to automate the entire queue. The finance team calculates eighteen thousand dollars in monthly labour cost and starts treating the full amount as savings. There is one problem. Processing a document is not the same as eliminating the cost of processing it. Some invoices still need people. Every invoice needs controls. The agent has operating costs. And the time released from a task does not automatically turn into cash. In the next few minutes, we will calculate the actual economics, compare three outcomes, and build a decision rule a finance leader can audit. Every figure in this example is hypothetical. The method is the lesson.

### 00:48 to 01:43 | Define the baseline
Start with the work, not the software. Six thousand invoices per month, five minutes of manual work per invoice. That is thirty thousand minutes, or five hundred hours. At a fully loaded labour rate of thirty-six dollars per hour, the monthly baseline is eighteen thousand dollars. Multiply by twelve and the annual baseline is two hundred sixteen thousand dollars. This is the reference cost, not the agent's benefit. Record the source of each input. Does five minutes come from a timed sample or a sales slide? Does thirty-six dollars include benefits and supervision? Do the six thousand invoices include duplicates, cancellations and exceptions? If your baseline is unreliable, every ROI percentage built on top of it is unreliable too. Put the baseline on screen before you discuss automation.

### 01:43 to 02:55 | Straight-through is the first variable
The vendor says ninety percent accuracy. That is not enough information to build a business case. Ask what percentage of invoices reaches a correct, approved endpoint without manual repair. We will call that the straight-through rate. For our base case, assume seventy-five percent. Four thousand five hundred invoices finish without the full manual process. The other fifteen hundred still require five minutes each. That is one hundred twenty-five hours of exception handling. At thirty-six dollars per hour, exception handling costs four thousand five hundred dollars a month. Now add a separate control. Suppose every invoice, including successful ones, receives forty-five seconds of human review or sampling-equivalent oversight. Six thousand times forty-five seconds equals seventy-five hours, or two thousand seven hundred dollars. In a real deployment, design the review model to match risk. Some workflows use targeted samples rather than review of every item. For this teaching example, the control assumption stays constant so the arithmetic stays transparent.

### 02:55 to 04:10 | The costs vendors leave off the slide
We now have seven thousand two hundred dollars in retained human work. Add eighteen hundred dollars each month for model calls, hosting and integration. Add twenty-two hundred dollars for maintenance, evaluation, exception-rule changes and operational support. Add a one-thousand-dollar monthly risk reserve for expected remediation and quality incidents. That reserve is a planning assumption, not a claim about industry incident rates. The post-automation monthly cost is twelve thousand two hundred dollars. Subtract that from the eighteen-thousand-dollar baseline. The model yields five thousand eight hundred dollars in monthly economic benefit, not eighteen thousand. That is about thirty-two percent of the baseline. It is still a potentially worthwhile project. It is simply not the result implied by a slide showing six thousand invoices multiplied by five minutes.

### 04:10 to 05:15 | Implementation and payback
Now include the cost of getting into production. Assume thirty thousand dollars in one-time integration, testing, process redesign, training and launch expense. Divide thirty thousand by five thousand eight hundred. Simple payback is about five point two months, provided the monthly benefit is realized and stable. Over twelve months, the recurring benefit totals sixty-nine thousand six hundred dollars. After the initial thirty thousand dollars, the first-year net economic benefit is thirty-nine thousand six hundred dollars. Relative to the thirty-thousand-dollar initial investment, that is a simple first-year net return of one hundred thirty-two percent. Do not confuse this with accounting profit, internal rate of return or a discounted cash flow. A serious investment memo would also model ramp-up, taxes, financing, depreciation where relevant, and timing of savings.

### 05:15 to 06:27 | The sensitivity test
Here is the number that changes the decision. If straight-through completion falls to sixty percent, two thousand four hundred invoices need the original five-minute manual process. That exception work costs seven thousand two hundred dollars. Keep the same two thousand seven hundred dollars in review and five thousand dollars in combined operating, maintenance and reserve costs. Total post-automation cost rises to fourteen thousand nine hundred dollars. Savings fall to three thousand one hundred dollars a month. Payback stretches to about nine point seven months. If completion reaches ninety percent, only six hundred invoices need manual processing, costing eighteen hundred dollars. With the other costs unchanged, the total becomes nine thousand five hundred dollars. Savings rise to eight thousand five hundred dollars a month. Payback drops to roughly three point five months. Same agent concept. Same baseline. Different exception rate. A vendor's headline model score does not replace measured straight-through results in your own workflow.

### 06:27 to 07:40 | Three separate scorecards
A responsible AI business case has three scorecards. First, quality: correct outputs, policy exceptions, rework, incident severity and the time needed to resolve errors. Second, economics: baseline cost, retained human effort, operating cost, implementation cost and the net benefit. Third, governance: who authorizes actions, which data the agent accesses, whether every consequential action is logged, and who stops the workflow when controls fail. These measures belong together. An agent with high throughput but poor auditability is not a successful deployment. The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology provides a voluntary AI risk-management framework and a generative AI profile. OWASP's 2026 agentic-security guidance identifies security risks in agents that plan and act through tools. These are useful reference points, not proof that any individual product is compliant.

### 07:40 to 08:43 | Capacity is not always cash
Now the accounting distinction most ROI videos miss. If the team saves hours but salaries and contractor bills remain unchanged, the company has released capacity. It has not automatically reduced cash spending. Released capacity still has value if staff handle more invoices, reduce backlog, improve service or avoid future hiring. Record those outcomes separately. Cash savings need evidence such as lower overtime, fewer external processing invoices or a documented change in staffing expenditure. Do not count the same hour twice as both a cash reduction and productivity gain. Ask finance to sign off on the value category before the pilot starts. Ask operations to sign off on the actual exception rate. Ask risk and compliance to sign off on the control design. And ask procurement to test the exit path, including data export and vendor dependency.

### 08:43 to 09:28 | Decision and close
Here is the decision rule. Run the agent on a representative sample. Measure the percentage of transactions completed correctly without repair. Time every human exception. Record review and maintenance effort. Recalculate the model with real costs. If the benefit survives the low-case scenario, the controls work, and a named owner accepts the residual risk, scale in stages. If the economics only work when every document is perfect, keep the pilot small. In this hypothetical example, the honest base case is five thousand eight hundred dollars a month, not eighteen thousand. Follow PrismBay AI for practical business systems with measurable outcomes. The model and assumptions belong in your decision memo, not buried in a vendor demonstration.
