## Opening | 0:00–0:50

A team tells its CFO: “Our AI assistant saves sixty hours every month.” The CFO asks one question: “Which expense disappeared?” Silence. This is where many AI pilots lose credibility. Time saved is not automatically cash saved. An AI system can create valuable capacity, improve turnaround, or reduce errors without reducing payroll by a dollar. Those are legitimate outcomes, but they belong in different columns. Today, I’ll build a thirty-day scorecard for an AI pilot, calculate a complete example, and show the decision rule for expanding, modifying, or stopping the project. You can copy the framework for document review, service requests, procurement, or internal operations.

On screen: TIME RELEASED ≠ CASH SAVED. Then: BASELINE → PILOT → LEDGER → DECISION.

## 1. Choose one narrow workflow | 0:50–2:00

Do not start with “AI across the business.” Choose a repeatable process with a named owner and a measurable output. For our example, a team handles one thousand supplier requests per month. Staff currently spend twelve minutes reviewing and routing each request. The workflow has a defined beginning, an approval point, and a recorded end. We will test AI assistance for triage and drafting. Humans still approve decisions and payments.

Before installing anything, define the boundaries. The assistant reads only approved fields, drafts a recommendation, and records supporting references. It cannot authorize payments, change supplier banking details, or send confidential documents to an unapproved destination. This matters because an apparent productivity gain is worthless if it introduces unacceptable financial or data exposure.

On screen: Scope / Owner / Input / Output / Approval / Prohibited actions.

## 2. Capture a defensible baseline | 2:00–3:15

Take a representative sample before the pilot. Record volume, mean and median handling time, the percentage of work returned for correction, exceptions, and the actual cash spent on overtime or external support. Use the mean or actual summed minutes for labor-hour arithmetic; keep the median to detect changes in typical case duration. Avoid cherry-picking easy cases. Segment simple requests from complex exceptions, because a model that performs well on routine tasks may still fail on the cases where judgment matters.

Our hypothetical baseline is one thousand requests times twelve minutes. That equals twelve thousand minutes, or two hundred labor hours. After introducing an AI-assisted draft, suppose average handling time falls to eight minutes. One thousand times eight minutes equals eight thousand minutes, or about one hundred thirty-three hours. The difference is sixty-six point seven hours of capacity per month.

Notice what I did not say. I did not call sixty-six point seven hours a cash saving. First, we must establish what happened to those hours.

On screen: 1,000 × 12 ÷ 60 = 200 hours. 1,000 × 8 ÷ 60 = 133.3 hours. Capacity released = 66.7 hours.

## 3. Maintain two separate benefit ledgers | 3:15–4:50

Ledger A measures capacity. At a fully loaded labor cost of thirty-five dollars per hour, sixty-six point seven hours represents about two thousand three hundred thirty-three dollars of potential labor capacity. The team might use that capacity to clear a backlog, serve more suppliers, or perform deeper checks. Useful, yes. Realized cash saving, not yet.

Ledger B records verified financial effects. Suppose the team previously paid for thirty hours of avoidable overtime per month and the pilot eliminates those thirty hours without reducing service quality. Thirty multiplied by thirty-five dollars equals one thousand fifty dollars in cash avoided. Record the payroll or invoice evidence. If no expense disappears, record zero cash savings even when capacity improves.

Do not double count the same hours. Do not multiply all hours saved by salary and add avoided overtime on top. Those measures overlap. The finance owner should sign off on the realized benefit, not merely the project team.

On screen: Capacity value $2,333. Verified overtime avoided $1,050. Different ledgers. Never add overlapping benefits.

## 4. Count the full cost of operation | 4:50–6:15

An AI pilot does not cost only the subscription price. Count model or software usage, integration support, review time, error correction, monitoring, and ongoing governance. For this example, assume four hundred twenty dollars per month for software and usage, three hundred for integration and maintenance, and two hundred forty for incremental quality review. The recurring cost is nine hundred sixty dollars per month.

Now the cash equation is simple. One thousand fifty dollars of verified overtime avoided minus nine hundred sixty dollars of recurring cost equals ninety dollars of monthly net cash benefit. That is a small positive number, not a spectacular return. If initial setup cost fifteen hundred dollars, the simple payback period at ninety dollars per month is roughly sixteen point seven months. A decision-maker may reasonably reject the pilot on cash return alone, or approve a limited extension because backlog reduction and service improvements have separately measured strategic value.

Never hide a weak financial case behind a dramatic time-saved graphic.

On screen: $1,050 - ($420 + $300 + $240) = $90/month. $1,500 ÷ $90 ≈ 16.7 months simple payback. All figures hypothetical.

## 5. Stress-test the assumptions | 6:15–7:35

What if staff adopt the tool less often than expected? What if corrections take longer? What if overtime is seasonal? Test at least three scenarios: expected, downside, and upside. In our downside scenario, only fifteen overtime hours disappear. Fifteen times thirty-five equals five hundred twenty-five dollars. Against nine hundred sixty dollars in operating cost, the monthly net is negative four hundred thirty-five dollars. The project destroys cash under those assumptions.

For an upside case, if sixty overtime hours genuinely disappear, the avoided cost is two thousand one hundred dollars, leaving one thousand one hundred forty dollars per month after recurring costs. At that level, fifteen hundred dollars of setup cost would be recovered in about one point three months. The same tool produces radically different economics depending on verified operational changes.

That is why a serious pilot measures outcomes rather than assuming every saved minute converts into money.

On screen: Downside −$435. Expected +$90. Upside +$1,140. Sensitivity, not prediction.

## 6. Quality and control are gates, not footnotes | 7:35–8:50

A pilot should not expand solely because average handling time improves. Establish hard limits for error rate, confidentiality incidents, unapproved actions, and unresolved exceptions. Sample outputs each week. Keep the human approval checkpoint. Log who reviewed a recommendation and which evidence supported it. Assign an owner who can pause the system if a serious failure occurs.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes responsible AI work around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. The OECD AI Principles emphasize accountability, oversight, and robustness. Those references support the discipline of monitoring and control, but they do not certify any individual product or this example.

Our proposed decision gate is straightforward. Expand only if measured cash economics or clearly documented capacity outcomes meet the agreed target, service quality does not deteriorate, and risk controls pass. Modify when the value is plausible but weak or inconsistent. Stop when the risk threshold fails or a credible route to value is absent.

On screen: FINANCIAL GATE / QUALITY GATE / RISK GATE. All three must pass.

## 7. The one-page scorecard | 8:50–9:55

At the end of thirty days, put seven fields on one page. Number one: cases processed. Number two: baseline and pilot handling time. Number three: capacity released. Number four: verified cash avoided or new contribution margin, with finance evidence. Number five: total recurring and one-time costs. Number six: quality and risk exceptions. Number seven: decision and accountable owner.

For our example, the report would say: one thousand cases, four minutes faster per case, sixty-six point seven hours of capacity released, one thousand fifty dollars of verified overtime avoided, nine hundred sixty dollars of monthly operating cost, and ninety dollars of monthly net cash benefit. Then list the actual error and control results. If those last results are missing, the business case is incomplete.

The lesson is not “AI always pays.” The lesson is that a thirty-day pilot should make the investment decision clearer. Measure the work, separate capacity from cash, include all costs, and retain human accountability.
