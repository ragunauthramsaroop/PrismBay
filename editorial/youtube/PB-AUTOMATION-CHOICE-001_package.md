## Original thesis
The most expensive AI mistake is often choosing an agent for a workflow that needs a rule, a form, or an ordinary approval queue. Evaluate three lanes: RULES, ASSISTANCE, AGENT. Choose the least complex system that reliably achieves the business outcome. The three-lane decision model and all examples are PrismBay editorial teaching tools, not official NIST standards. Every monetary and performance number below is hypothetical.

## Complete narration script

### 00:00–01:00 | Cold open: a $900 bill for an $80 improvement
Your team handles twelve hundred supplier requests each month. A vendor proposes an AI agent that reads every request, makes decisions, and routes the work. The demo looks excellent. But suppose the agent saves only eighty dollars a month compared with a simpler workflow, before implementation, monitoring, and mistakes. Would you still deploy it?

Today I will show you a practical three-lane decision system: ordinary rules, AI assistance, and a limited agent. We will apply the same business case to all three. Then we will calculate the real incremental value and identify the point where an agent earns the right to exist. The objective is not more AI. It is a better operating system for the business.

Visual: split-screen: glossy agent dashboard vs simple queue. Reveal "$80 / MONTH BEFORE SETUP" with "illustrative calculation" visible.

### 01:00–02:20 | Lane one: rules
Start by asking whether the process is predictable. A supplier request arrives. Required fields are present or missing. The vendor exists in an approved register or does not. The invoice amount falls within an established range or exceeds it. These are ordinary checks.

If a stable rule answers the question, use a rule. A form can reject missing attachments. A workflow can route an exception. A database can validate a supplier identifier. None of those actions needs a model to improvise a decision.

The important distinction is between repetitive work and ambiguous work. Repetition does not automatically justify AI. In fact, stable repetition often makes conventional automation more attractive because outcomes are easier to test and explain.

Visual: original decision tree: predictable input → deterministic rule → pass or exception queue. Show two sample requests and the same rule applied consistently.

### 02:20–03:40 | Lane two: assistance
Now consider the twenty percent of requests that do not fit the standard path. One includes an unusual delivery clause. Another has a supplier letter written in a different format. A third contains conflicting descriptions. A language model might summarize these cases, extract proposed fields, or draft a clarification.

That is AI assistance. The model helps a named employee interpret information, but the employee retains the decision and the system retains its original permissions. This is often enough.

Do not let a model turn an uncertain reading into a hidden automatic approval. Ask it to cite the passage it relied on, mark uncertainty, and send conflicting evidence to a reviewer. The value is faster comprehension and better documentation, not an unearned claim of autonomous accuracy.

Visual: original side-by-side document excerpts, highlighted evidence, review queue. Use synthetic fictional supplier names and watermark "DEMONSTRATION DATA".

### 03:40–04:55 | Lane three: a limited agent
An agent is more appropriate when the work genuinely requires choosing between steps, using approved tools, checking intermediate results, and adapting to changing conditions. Picture a service request that requires reading an approved record, querying a second system, checking the result, and assembling a proposed next action.

Even here, give the agent the narrowest possible tool access. Let it gather evidence and prepare a proposal. Do not give it the credentials to change bank details or release a payment. A separate authorized reviewer and server-side control must govern those actions.

The decision is not "AI versus no AI." It is whether dynamic orchestration delivers an outcome that a simpler design cannot deliver reliably at lower total cost.

Visual: three lanes with different execution paths. Rules: straight line. Assistance: model → human review. Agent: plan → allowlisted tools → verify → approval → controlled action.

### 04:55–06:50 | The actual financial test
Let's calculate a deliberately hypothetical case. Twelve hundred requests per month. Eight minutes of manual handling each. At thirty dollars per loaded labor hour, the baseline is one hundred sixty hours, or four thousand eight hundred dollars of labor capacity per month. Capacity is not automatically a cash saving.

Option one is rules plus human exception handling. Suppose eighty percent of requests now need two minutes, while the remaining twenty percent still need eight. That is sixty-four hours of manual handling. At thirty dollars an hour, the labor value is nineteen hundred twenty dollars. Add two hundred dollars a month for the workflow platform. Total modeled operating cost: twenty-one hundred twenty dollars. Modeled reduction from baseline: twenty-six hundred eighty dollars.

Option two adds AI assistance only for the twenty percent of exceptions. The standard eighty percent still takes two minutes. Suppose assisted exceptions take four minutes of human review. That totals forty-eight hours, or fourteen hundred forty dollars in labor value. Add six hundred dollars for the combined workflow and AI usage, oversight, and monitoring. Total: two thousand forty dollars. Reduction from baseline: twenty-seven hundred sixty dollars.

Compare the two realistic alternatives, not only each alternative against the manual baseline. The AI-assisted workflow improves the modeled monthly result by eighty dollars. Eighty dollars is not enough to justify a large setup bill or unmanaged risk. Unless the assisted workflow also improves quality, turnaround, or scalability, the simpler option wins.

Visual: show calculations line by line. Baseline 1,200 × 8 ÷ 60 = 160 h; 160 × $30 = $4,800. Rules 1,200 × (0.8 × 2 + 0.2 × 8) ÷ 60 = 64 h; 64 × $30 + $200 = $2,120. Hybrid 1,200 × (0.8 × 2 + 0.2 × 4) ÷ 60 = 48 h; 48 × $30 + $600 = $2,040. Incremental difference = $80. Label every figure HYPOTHETICAL. Explain that labor-value reductions require separate cash validation.

### 06:50–08:00 | The four questions that change the decision
Question one: how much variation exists in the input? Stable structured data favors rules. Ambiguous unstructured evidence might benefit from AI assistance.

Question two: what happens when the system is wrong? If a mistake changes money, legal obligations, personal data, or external communications, keep an independent approval boundary.

Question three: how often do exceptions occur? If almost every request is standard, an agent is solving a problem that barely exists. If exceptions dominate, investigate why the underlying process is so inconsistent before automating the inconsistency.

Question four: what is the fully loaded cost? Include integration, model calls, employee review, monitoring, rework, vendor dependency, data controls, and incident recovery. A monthly API bill is not the total cost of an AI workflow.

Visual: custom four-quadrant scorecard: INPUT VARIATION, ERROR CONSEQUENCE, EXCEPTION RATE, FULL COST.

### 08:00–09:15 | A thirty-day proof before scale
For week one, record one hundred real but appropriately protected cases, including difficult exceptions. Establish baseline handling time, rework, and turnaround. For week two, test ordinary rules against the same cases. For week three, add assistance only where rules fail, and require reviewers to record every correction. In week four, compare the outcomes and decide whether any narrow agent action is justified.

Measure five things: completion quality, manual minutes, verified cash effect, exception rate, and unauthorized action attempts. Do not use the same examples for building the workflow and judging its success. Test edge cases and reversibility. An agent that saves time but produces unauthorized actions fails the pilot.

If the results support AI assistance but not autonomous action, stop at assistance. This is a successful design decision, not a failed AI strategy.

Visual: four-week timeline with original evidence cards. Red "STOP" for unauthorized action; green "CONTINUE" only when predeclared quality and control thresholds pass.

### 09:15–09:55 | Executive decision
Here is the rule I would put in front of an investment committee. Choose rules when the process is predictable. Choose assistance when people need help interpreting evidence. Choose an agent only when changing conditions require tool-based decisions and the incremental value exceeds the full incremental cost and risk.

Take one workflow and compare all three lanes before approving a technology purchase. PrismBay AI helps founders, operators, and executives implement practical AI business systems. Subscribe for the next episode on testing AI workflows before they touch live operations.
