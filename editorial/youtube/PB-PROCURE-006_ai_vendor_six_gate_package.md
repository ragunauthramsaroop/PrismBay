## Original premise
A compelling AI demo establishes that a tool performs a selected example. It does not establish production reliability, data rights, financial return, human accountability, or an affordable exit. PrismBay's original six-gate procurement test is a practical teaching device, not a statutory standard, certification, or framework endorsed by the cited institutions.

Six gates: OUTCOME / DATA / AUTHORITY / EVIDENCE / ECONOMICS / EXIT.
Decision: PASS, REPAIR, or REJECT. Any mandatory security, privacy, or authorization failure blocks production deployment regardless of the total score.

## Finished narration and original on-screen direction

### 0:00–0:55 | Cold open: the perfect demo
An AI vendor shows you a remarkable demonstration. The assistant reads a contract, finds a problem, writes a response, and finishes in seconds. Your team applauds. Then the vendor sends a twelve-month contract.

What did the demonstration prove? One selected task worked under selected conditions. It did not prove the tool handles your messy files, protects your customer data, respects your approval rules, or saves more money than it costs.

Before signing, run six gates. We will apply them to a fictional supplier-support workflow, calculate the real operating economics, and finish with a decision sheet your team can use. No vendor names and no sponsored recommendation.

VISUAL: custom side-by-side of a polished DEMO card and a blank BUSINESS CASE ledger. Six distinct gates appear one at a time. No stock presenter or generic logo intro.

### 0:55–2:00 | Gate 1: OUTCOME, not features
Start with one business outcome. For example: reduce the time spent checking whether supplier requests contain the required attachments. Specify the owner, the workflow start and finish, monthly case volume, current handling time, error rate, and what a successful result would change.

A weak objective says, "Deploy an AI assistant." A measurable objective says, "Reduce average manual triage from nine minutes to six, without increasing correction rates or exposing sensitive records."

Set a deadline and the stopping rule before a vendor runs the pilot. Ask whether a simpler rules-based system could solve the same problem at lower cost. If the answer is yes, compare that option first.

VISUAL: animated transformation from "BUY AI" to a complete measurement card with OWNER, BASELINE, TARGET, QUALITY LIMIT.

### 2:00–3:10 | Gate 2: DATA, with evidence
Ask what information the product receives, where it goes, which subcontractors process it, how long it stays, whether customer inputs train any models, and what deletion means in practice. Do not accept a sales slide as a substitute for written terms and technical documentation.

Classify the pilot data before uploading it. Start with synthetic or appropriately de-identified records when possible. Confirm access controls, storage regions where relevant, incident reporting, retention settings, and export restrictions. In regulated settings, legal and security owners should determine the applicable requirements.

If the vendor cannot explain the data flow or the contract contradicts the demonstration, stop the procurement until the gap is resolved.

VISUAL: original data-flow diagram with SOURCE -> PROCESSOR -> SUBPROCESSOR -> STORAGE -> DELETION. Red highlight on an unexplained arrow.

### 3:10–4:20 | Gate 3: AUTHORITY, not a clever prompt
Separate what the system reads from what it can change. In our supplier example, the tool may read approved request fields, classify missing attachments, and draft an internal reply. It should not create vendors, change banking details, release payments, or send an external message without independent authorization.

Ask the vendor to demonstrate permissions, not merely describe them. Show a denied action. Show an expired approval. Show what happens when a document tries to instruct the agent to ignore the rules. Credentials, tool access, transaction limits, and reviewer roles should be enforced outside the model's text instructions.

PrismBay's permission map is OBSERVE, DRAFT, PROPOSE, EXECUTE. Grant EXECUTE only for narrow, reversible actions with a clear owner, log, and recovery path.

VISUAL: four-level permission grid, then a real red STOP on a proposed supplier bank-account change. Label the scenario hypothetical.

### 4:20–5:35 | Gate 4: EVIDENCE from your workload
Do not score a model on the vendor's five favorite examples. Build a representative test set with routine, difficult, incomplete, and misleading inputs. Hold out cases the vendor has not seen. Define the correct outcome and score each case.

Record task completion, false approvals, corrections, reviewer time, latency, and system failures. Ask for repeat runs. A system that produces the right answer once but fails unpredictably is not production-ready.

For consequential decisions, require a human reviewer. Test a malicious instruction embedded in an attachment, an approval altered after sign-off, and a timeout that might trigger a duplicate action. Keep the evidence and failed cases. If the tool fails a mandatory control test, repair the control and retest before expanding permissions.

VISUAL: original test board of 20 fictional cases: ROUTINE / EDGE / MALICIOUS / FAILURE. Show one failed test and the corrective control, never an invented real-world benchmark.

### 5:35–7:10 | Gate 5: ECONOMICS, including the hidden work
Now the arithmetic. Suppose the company handles six hundred supplier requests a month. Baseline manual triage averages nine minutes. In the pilot, the average drops to five. Six hundred times four minutes equals twenty-four hundred minutes, or forty hours of capacity released.

Forty hours is not forty hours of payroll savings. The team may spend the time clearing a backlog. Record that as capacity and service value. Cash savings require evidence that an expense was avoided or a new contribution margin was earned.

Assume the company verifies that ten hours of paid overtime disappeared at thirty-five dollars per hour. That is three hundred fifty dollars of avoided cash cost. The vendor charges three hundred dollars per month. Additional review and monitoring cost two hundred dollars. Net recurring cash impact: three hundred fifty minus five hundred equals negative one hundred fifty dollars per month.

The pilot may still improve turnaround, but the CFO should not approve it as a cash-saving project. Compare a cheaper automation option, renegotiate the subscription, reduce monitoring burden without weakening controls, or measure a legitimate service benefit. Include setup costs, annual price increases, usage limits, and failure-handling costs.

VISUAL: two independent ledgers. CAPACITY: 600 x (9-5) / 60 = 40 hours. CASH: 10 x $35 - ($300 + $200) = -$150/month. Prominent "HYPOTHETICAL, NOT A PERFORMANCE CLAIM".

### 7:10–8:15 | Gate 6: EXIT before entry
What happens if the vendor doubles prices, changes a model, removes a feature, suffers an outage, or you decide to leave? Ask how you export data and logs, which file formats you receive, how deletion is verified, and how long the transition takes.

Identify the operational fallback. Who handles supplier requests while the system is offline? Can the team retrieve outstanding approvals? Will the vendor help with migration? A low entry price with a costly exit is not necessarily a low total cost.

Put the exit conditions in the agreement before the organization becomes dependent on the tool.

VISUAL: two timelines, onboarding versus offboarding. A highlighted EXIT COST cell, then a reversible workflow diagram.

### 8:15–9:35 | Score and decide
Here is the complete decision sheet. For each gate, mark PASS when evidence meets your pre-agreed criterion, REPAIR when the gap is fixable with a named owner and deadline, and REJECT when the vendor cannot satisfy a non-negotiable requirement.

Do not average away a serious privacy, security, or payment-control failure. If a mandatory gate fails, production deployment stops even if the demo is impressive.

Our hypothetical supplier pilot releases forty hours of capacity but loses one hundred fifty dollars per month in verified recurring cash terms. If the business objective is cash reduction, the economic gate fails. If the objective is faster response time, run a controlled extension only after measuring service outcomes and passing every mandatory control.

The executive question is not "Which AI vendor has the most features?" It is "Which workflow improves, what evidence proves the improvement, who owns the risk, and how do we exit?"

VISUAL: original six-gate scorecard. OUTCOME PASS, DATA PASS, AUTHORITY PASS, EVIDENCE REPAIR, ECONOMICS REPAIR, EXIT PASS. Example status: LIMITED PILOT, NOT APPROVED FOR PRODUCTION.
