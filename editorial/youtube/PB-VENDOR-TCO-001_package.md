## Finished voiceover and scene script

### 0:00–0:55 | Cold open | Two prices, one decision

A vendor quotes your company two hundred ninety-nine dollars a month for an AI tool. Your team celebrates because the price looks low. Thirty days later, the tool is costing far more than the subscription. Not because the vendor lied. Because your business counted the license and forgot the work around it.

Here is a fictional but fully calculated example. A two hundred ninety-nine dollar license becomes two thousand twenty-two dollars of monthly operating cost when we include seats, usage, integration, human review, training, and data-handling overhead. None of these numbers is a market quote. They are teaching assumptions. I will show every line, then explain how to decide whether the tool is worth keeping.

### 0:55–2:15 | The seven-line cost ledger

Start with line one, the base platform subscription: two hundred ninety-nine dollars. Line two, twelve employee seats at twenty-nine dollars each: three hundred forty-eight. Line three, estimated metered API or workflow usage: one hundred twenty.

Line four is implementation work. Suppose you spend fourteen thousand four hundred dollars connecting the system to existing workflows and spread that investment over thirty months for planning. That is four hundred eighty dollars per month. Cash timing differs: the implementation bill might arrive upfront, while the monthly number is an amortized comparison measure.

Line five is human oversight. Suppose a reviewer spends fifteen hours each month checking outputs and exceptions at an assumed fully loaded labor cost of thirty-five dollars per hour. That is five hundred twenty-five dollars of capacity consumed. Unless this work creates additional cash spending, do not call the full amount a cash expense.

Line six, onboarding and training allocated to each month: one hundred sixty. Line seven, data exports, backup handling, or compliance administration: ninety.

Add the lines: two hundred ninety-nine plus three hundred forty-eight plus one hundred twenty plus four hundred eighty plus five hundred twenty-five plus one hundred sixty plus ninety. Total: two thousand twenty-two dollars per month in the planning model.

[Original visual: seven-row cost ledger; sum calculated live. Split CAPACITY/ACCOUNTING MODEL from CASH OUTFLOW to prevent misleading ROI.]

### 2:15–3:30 | The license is not the workflow

An AI product might look impressive in a demo and still require people to correct errors, resolve access requests, prepare documents, and re-enter information into another system. Those steps are part of the workflow. A cheaper license does not solve a more expensive process.

Map the complete task from incoming request to verified outcome. Count how many human minutes remain after deployment. Count how often the AI answer is rejected. Track any extra review steps introduced by the tool. If the team saves five minutes drafting but adds six minutes of checking, the workflow is slower.

Ask for evidence from a pilot using your own tasks, not only benchmark slides from the vendor. Document the task mix, sample size, exception rate, and baseline. This is consistent with the voluntary NIST AI risk-management emphasis on mapping, measuring, and managing real deployment risk, but our specific worksheet is a PrismBay teaching tool.

[Original visual: swimlane showing OLD WORKFLOW vs NEW WORKFLOW with real review handoffs and time boxes.]

### 3:30–4:55 | Value has three different meanings

First, time released. Suppose eight hundred tasks a month become three minutes faster. Eight hundred times three divided by sixty equals forty hours of capacity. Useful, yes. But forty hours of capacity does not automatically mean forty hours of payroll reduction.

Second, verified cash effects. If reduced overtime, avoided contractor invoices, or eliminated software subscriptions genuinely save the business one thousand dollars monthly, count those savings once. Never count the same forty hours as both a payroll saving and a capacity benefit unless there is distinct evidence.

Third, service quality. Did customers receive answers sooner? Did accuracy improve? Did exceptions decrease? A workflow that runs faster but sends incorrect financial instructions is not a success.

Our fictional tool has two thousand twenty-two dollars of monthly modeled cost. Suppose documented cash savings are one thousand dollars. The simple recurring cash comparison is negative one thousand twenty-two dollars before adjusting for the distinction between accounting allocations and cash timing. The vendor might still be worth keeping for measurable quality or revenue gains, but leaders must state those benefits separately instead of inventing an ROI percentage.

[Original visual: three benefit lanes: CAPACITY, CASH, QUALITY. Red double-count warning. Separate cash flow from allocated TCO.]

### 4:55–6:05 | Vendor proof: request five artifacts

Before approving a longer contract, request five things.

One: a pricing schedule showing seats, metered usage, rate changes, and usage caps.

Two: evidence from a pilot using your actual workflow, including accuracy and failure cases.

Three: the access and data map. What information enters the system, where does it go, and which users or agents can act on it?

Four: incident and recovery documentation. What happens when a model, integration, or external provider fails?

Five: an exit plan. How do you export your data, revoke credentials, remove integrations, and verify deletion where the contract and law require it?

These requests translate broad risk principles into a procurement conversation. The NIST AI RMF is voluntary guidance. OWASP identifies prompt injection and excessive agency among LLM application risks. Neither source certifies an individual vendor because the vendor mentions the framework.

[Original visual: five real-looking but fictional evidence folders, stamped RECEIVED, MISSING, or NEEDS TEST.]

### 6:05–7:25 | Test the failure, not only the success

Run a small pilot with fictional or authorized data. Give the AI an ordinary supplier request and measure its performance. Then give it a confusing request with a changed bank account, a missing attachment, and an instruction embedded in a document asking the system to bypass approval.

A safe design treats document instructions as untrusted data. It should not change a vendor bank account, release funds, or send confidential records merely because a document asked. Confirm these boundaries through actual tool permissions and independent checks. A polished model response is not proof of technical control.

Now disable the integration in a sandbox. Check whether work queues recover, records remain consistent, and reviewers know how to resume manually. Record failures as part of the procurement evidence, not as embarrassing exceptions to hide.

[Original visual: side-by-side PASS and FAIL pilot transactions, with explicit simulated labels.]

### 7:25–8:40 | The renewal decision

At day thirty, put three columns in front of leadership. Column one is full modeled cost with cash timing shown separately. Column two is documented benefit, split into capacity, cash, and quality. Column three is unresolved risk: data access, failure recovery, vendor dependence, and contract exit.

Keep the product only if the expected benefit is credible, the operational controls pass, and the organization has an accountable owner. Modify the workflow if the model is helpful but human review consumes too much time. Stop expansion if a serious control fails.

Here is the takeaway: an AI subscription is a line item. An AI business system is an operating model. The purchase decision belongs to the second number, not the first.

## Seven-line arithmetic audit

Base: 299
Seats: 12 x 29 = 348
Usage: 120
Implementation allocation: 14,400 / 30 = 480
Human review capacity: 15 x 35 = 525
Training allocation: 160
Data handling: 90
TOTAL MODELED MONTHLY COST = 299 + 348 + 120 + 480 + 525 + 160 + 90 = 2,022
Pilot capacity released: 800 x 3 / 60 = 40 hours
Illustrative documented cash savings: 1,000/month
Difference against modeled TCO: 1,000 - 2,022 = -1,022/month. This is NOT a cash-flow calculation because allocations and capacity cost are mixed into modeled TCO. Do not label it net cash effect or realized ROI.
