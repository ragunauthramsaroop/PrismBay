## Positioning and original thesis
The real procurement test is not whether a demo answers a question. It is whether the buyer retains control of data, permissions, evidence, costs and operations when the contract ends. PrismBay's five-test EXIT framework is an original teaching device, not a NIST standard or legal advice:
E = Export and portability.
X = eXecution authority and access removal.
I = Inspection of records and incidents.
T = Total cost of transition.
+ Continuity rehearsal = the fifth test, separate from the mnemonic.

## Full narration script

### 0:00–0:55 | The procurement question nobody asks
A vendor shows you an impressive AI demo. It classifies supplier requests, drafts responses and promises fewer manual steps. The subscription is affordable. The implementation looks easy. So the team signs a twelve-month contract.

Now move the clock forward. The vendor changes its pricing, your risk team asks for audit evidence, or the company decides to switch providers. Where are your original files? How do you export decisions and approval records? Who revokes the agent's access? How long will the old and new systems run together?

The best question in an AI buying decision is not only, "Does it work today?" Ask, "What happens when we need to leave?"

Here are five tests a small business or enterprise team can run before approving an AI vendor. I will also show the numbers behind a seemingly cheap subscription.

Visual: premium product demo card transforms into an EXIT checklist. No stock footage, no animated logo intro.

### 0:55–2:15 | Test one: export a usable sample
Ask the vendor to export a representative set of your own test records. You need more than a PDF screenshot. Request the underlying data, attachments, record IDs, timestamps and relationships needed to reconstruct the workflow in another system.

Use synthetic sample records rather than real confidential customer information during early evaluation. Open the exported files without the vendor's application. Check whether you can identify each original input, the output produced, the reviewer and the final disposition.

A vendor might reasonably restrict export of its proprietary model weights or internal technology. That is different from withholding your business records. Define exactly which artifacts your organization needs to keep and verify the format before you sign.

On screen: 25 mock records → export → independent viewer. Highlight three failures: missing attachments, missing IDs, unreadable format.

### 2:15–3:30 | Test two: revoke the agent's authority
List every system the AI integration can read or change. Examples include a help desk, document store, procurement database and email account. For each connection, document the identity used, the scope of permissions, who approves access and how to revoke it.

The exit test is simple. Disable the integration in a test environment and confirm that API calls fail. Rotate or revoke credentials, remove webhooks and disable any scheduled jobs. Do not rely on deleting a chat or telling the model to stop.

An assistant that summarizes invoices does not need payment credentials. An assistant that drafts a supplier email does not need permission to send messages automatically. These are application-level boundaries, not polite instructions.

On screen: ACCESS MAP with four systems. A visible REVOKE button turns the permission links red. Label as a simulated test.

### 3:30–4:50 | Test three: inspect the evidence trail
Imagine a supplier disputes an automated recommendation from last month. Can you reconstruct what happened?

Ask for a sample audit record showing the input reference, time, action, system identity, policy check, human reviewer where required, outcome and error or rollback event. Test the search and export process. Agree on how long evidence remains available and what happens after termination.

You do not need to expose private model reasoning to prove that an approval occurred. You do need records that support accountability. For consequential actions, insist on a separate authorization record rather than an informal chat message.

On screen: request ID 0042, proposed action, approved by role, execution result. All records are fictional.

### 4:50–6:10 | Test four: calculate total transition cost
Here is a hypothetical offer. Vendor A costs three hundred dollars less per month than Vendor B. Over twelve months, the visible subscription difference is three thousand six hundred dollars.

Now suppose leaving Vendor A requires a five-thousand-dollar data conversion and workflow rebuild. Assume this cost is a credible, documented migration estimate, not a fee the vendor necessarily charges. The first-year comparison changes. Three thousand six hundred dollars of subscription savings minus five thousand dollars of transition expense equals negative one thousand four hundred dollars.

That is not a prediction. It shows why the cheaper monthly price does not always mean the cheaper ownership path.

Your cost model should include implementation, monitoring, human review, data export, dual-running, staff retraining and exit work. Use realistic scenarios. Avoid adding the same expense twice. Ask procurement and finance to review the assumptions.

On screen: $300 × 12 = $3,600. $3,600 − $5,000 = −$1,400. Label "Hypothetical example, not a vendor quote."

### 6:10–7:20 | Test five: rehearse continuity
A termination clause is useful. A successful recovery drill is stronger evidence.

Pick one business process and run a controlled rehearsal. Export the sample data, switch to a manual or alternate workflow, verify approvals, and measure how long normal service takes to resume. Record the gaps.

For a customer-support system, the fallback might be a shared queue with assigned owners. For procurement, it might be a restricted spreadsheet and an established approval path. Keep the fallback secure. Never bypass normal financial controls in the name of continuity.

Define who owns the decision to stop an AI workflow, what conditions trigger a pause and how the organization confirms that access is gone. The objective is not perfect uptime. It is a tested path back to accountable operations.

On screen: AI queue → pause → manual controlled queue → restore. Stopwatch graphic with no invented recovery time.

### 7:20–8:35 | The one-page buyer decision
Place the five test results in one table. Export: passed or failed, with sample file evidence. Authority: passed or failed, with credential revocation logs. Inspection: passed or failed, with audit sample. Transition economics: scenario range and owner-approved assumptions. Continuity: rehearsal date, findings and accountable owner.

Separate deal-breakers from negotiable improvements. Missing a reversible label is not the same as an AI system retaining unauthorized payment access. A buyer should not average a critical control failure into a favorable overall score.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework includes voluntary guidance on third-party and supply-chain risks. OWASP's 2026 agentic security reporting illustrates why actual tool permissions and independent controls deserve testing. Neither organization has evaluated or endorsed this PrismBay framework.

Before signing, ask the vendor to demonstrate each test against a small, approved pilot. Record the evidence in the procurement file.

### 8:35–9:05 | Payoff and channel promise
An AI tool is easier to trust when the buyer knows how to export its records, remove its access, inspect decisions, estimate exit costs and keep operating during a change.

Do not buy a demo. Buy a workflow you control.
