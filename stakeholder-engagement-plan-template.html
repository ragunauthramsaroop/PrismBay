A free working approach

A stakeholder list records names. A working engagement plan connects each relationship to a legitimate purpose, accountable owner, appropriate contact cadence and documented response. The distinction matters when several teams are meeting the same people, when commitments are made in public, or when an organization needs to explain how feedback influenced a decision.

1. Define the decision that engagement can influence

Write the operational or policy decision in one sentence. Separate information-sharing from genuine consultation: invite feedback only where it can affect the outcome. Identify topics that cannot be changed and disclose them early. This prevents stakeholders from being asked to attend meetings whose outcomes are already predetermined.

2. Group stakeholders by relationship and interest

Include communities, suppliers, customers, employees, regulators and affected groups where relevant. Record why each group has an interest, not merely how influential it is. Assess possible vulnerability, access barriers and who might otherwise be excluded. Avoid reducing people to a single numerical power score.

3. Choose engagement formats before setting a timetable

A town-hall meeting, confidential interview, supplier briefing and written consultation answer different needs. Specify accessibility, language, confidentiality and notice periods. For high-impact decisions, combine at least two channels so people unable to attend a meeting can still contribute.

4. Give every meeting a decision owner

Name the relationship owner and the person authorized to make commitments. Capture open questions, follow-up dates and when an issue requires escalation. If an action depends on legal, technical or budget approval, record it as proposed rather than promised.

5. Maintain a commitments register

For each commitment, record the exact wording, meeting or correspondence source, approving owner, due date, current evidence and closure condition. Do not mark a concern resolved merely because a reply was sent; the affected party may still disagree.

6. Report feedback and outstanding disagreements

Issue a short 'what we heard and what changed' brief. Distinguish actions taken, decisions deferred and requests that cannot be met, with reasons. Give stakeholders a route to correct the record or challenge a decision without retaliation.

7. Review monthly and after material changes

Audit overdue commitments, unresolved grievances, repeated requests and groups that have not been reached. Update the plan when a project scope changes, new risks emerge or engagement creates an unintended burden.

Frequently asked questions

Is a stakeholder register the same as an engagement plan?

No. A register identifies the relationship and relevant context. A plan adds objectives, engagement methods, accountable owners, timing, feedback handling and measurable follow-up.
