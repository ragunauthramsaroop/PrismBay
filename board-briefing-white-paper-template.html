A free working approach

Executives do not need a longer report when the decision, evidence or uncertainty is unclear. A defensible white paper separates independently sourced findings from management assumptions, identifies alternative explanations and ends with an explicit decision request. Its companion board brief should preserve that reasoning while making the action and remaining risk easy to examine.

1. Define the reader's actual decision

Begin with the decision, deadline, options and authority required. State the consequences of delay and which facts could reasonably change the recommendation. If the assignment is exploratory, say so instead of manufacturing a yes-or-no conclusion.

2. Build the source ledger before drafting

Capture original source title, publisher, author where known, publication date, retrieval date, stable URL or document reference, scope and material limitations. Attach every quantitative assertion to its precise source. Mark unpublished company information as confidential and follow authorization requirements.

3. Separate evidence from estimates and judgment

Create explicit labels for verified facts, calculations, assumptions, scenarios and recommendations. When two credible sources disagree, explain differences in methodology and population rather than quietly selecting a convenient number. Never cite a general homepage for a claim that comes from a specific report or table.

4. Show assumptions and adverse scenarios

An executive paper should disclose the assumptions under which its preferred option works and what happens if they fail. Identify alternatives, downside exposures, dependencies and the cost of corrective actions. Use ranges rather than unwarranted precision when the input data are uncertain.

5. Put the conclusion and next decision in one page

A board brief should answer: What is changing? What decision is requested? What evidence supports it? What alternatives were evaluated? What cannot yet be verified? Who owns the next action and by when? Keep lengthy technical analysis as linked supporting material.

6. Run an evidence and publication audit

Check every number, date, quotation, citation, chart source, permissions statement and potential conflict of interest. Verify that confidential materials were approved for circulation. Use a fresh reviewer who did not prepare the first draft.

7. Version the approved record

Retain the approved manuscript, data workbook and signed review record together. Record material post-approval changes with version dates so later readers can distinguish original conclusions from subsequently discovered information.

Is a white paper the same as a board memo?

No. A white paper can develop the full evidence and technical analysis. A board memo focuses on the governing decision, material evidence, alternatives and implementation accountability.
