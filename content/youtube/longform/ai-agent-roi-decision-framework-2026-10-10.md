## Original narration script

Your AI agent saves sixty-seven hours every month. Your team celebrates. Your dashboard turns green. And yet your business might be losing more than a thousand dollars a month on the same automation. This is not an argument against AI. It is a problem with how organizations calculate returns.

I want to walk through a simple example. All figures are hypothetical. They are not a claim about any company, vendor, or model. At the end, you will have a break-even test for deciding whether an AI workflow deserves a bigger budget.

First, distinguish three things: time saved, productive capacity created, and financial value realized. They are not synonyms. An employee who finishes a task four minutes earlier has freed up four minutes. That is time. Multiply across the team and you have capacity. But unless the organization converts that capacity into lower costs, additional throughput, better service, or another measurable result, the accounting value might be zero.

Here is the scenario. A company processes one thousand routine tasks every month. An AI-assisted workflow reduces handling time by four minutes per task. One thousand multiplied by four gives four thousand minutes. Divide by sixty and you get sixty-six point seven hours. Round it to sixty-seven hours of capacity.

Assume the fully loaded labor rate is thirty dollars an hour. Sixty-six point seven hours multiplied by thirty gives approximately two thousand dollars of theoretical capacity value per month. Notice my wording. Two thousand dollars is a ceiling under these assumptions, not a deposit in your bank account.

Now we count the cost of operating the system. In our illustrative case, infrastructure and model usage cost three hundred fifty dollars monthly. Human verification, exception handling and review cost six hundred. Integration support, monitoring and maintenance cost five hundred. Total recurring incremental cost: fourteen hundred fifty dollars.

If the company converts every dollar of potential capacity into real business value, the net monthly benefit is two thousand minus fourteen hundred fifty, or five hundred fifty dollars. Divide five hundred fifty by fourteen hundred fifty, and the modeled return on incremental operating cost is about thirty-eight percent. That is the optimistic case, and it still excludes one-time implementation expense.

Now the uncomfortable question: what if the company realizes only twenty percent of the theoretical benefit? Perhaps the saved minutes are fragmented. Perhaps the team has no additional demand. Perhaps staff still need to review every output. Twenty percent of two thousand is four hundred dollars of realized value. Four hundred minus fourteen hundred fifty is negative one thousand fifty dollars per month. The team saved sixty-seven hours on paper while the business lost money on the modeled operating basis.

This is why a slide showing hours saved is not enough. It describes efficiency. It does not establish return.

Let us calculate the break-even point. Divide fourteen hundred fifty dollars in monthly costs by two thousand dollars in potential value. The result is seventy-two point five percent. In this example, the company must realize at least seventy-two point five percent of the theoretical capacity value merely to cover recurring operating costs. Below that, the model is negative. Above that, it begins to generate net value. The hurdle rises when you include setup costs, retraining, security reviews, change management, or capital charges.

What counts as realized value? Start with verifiable expense reductions, such as an avoided outsourced processing invoice. Next, consider additional work completed using the same staffing and whether the added output creates measurable contribution margin. Then examine reduced errors, fewer rework cycles, shorter lead times, and better customer outcomes. For each claim, identify a baseline, an owner, a unit of measurement, and the evidence. Avoid adding the same benefit twice.

For example, if the system reduces handling time and also reduces rework, make sure the four minutes of time savings did not already include rework time. If human review is essential to maintain quality, include the review labor in operating cost. If failures are expensive, measure the cost of the failures, not just their frequency. A workflow with a ninety-eight percent success rate can still be a bad investment if the two percent of exceptions cause major losses.

Now build a thirty-day pilot. During the first week, measure the manual process without changing the definitions midway through the test. Record completed tasks, average handling time, error rate, rework minutes, cost per completed task, and exceptions. In the second week, run AI alongside the current process and compare results on the same task mix. In weeks three and four, introduce controlled automation only for low-risk tasks, with an explicit human escalation route.

The unit of analysis is a successfully completed task, not a generated response. Track cost per accepted output, end-to-end cycle time, human intervention minutes, failures, and realized financial value. Break the numbers down by task category. One workflow might produce excellent returns on structured extraction while failing on ambiguous approvals. Do not hide the losing category inside the winning average.

There is also a governance test. Who approves the use case? What data may enter the model? What happens when the model gives a confident but wrong answer? Who can stop the automation? What audit record proves who reviewed an exception? These questions should appear before deployment, not after an incident.

The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology organizes its voluntary AI Risk Management Framework around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. It is not a substitute for local legal requirements. It is a useful structure for assigning accountability, defining the intended use, evaluating performance, and managing residual risk. For a business buyer, this means evaluation and controls belong in the operating model and therefore in the ROI model.

At the end of thirty days, hold a go, modify, or stop review. Go if the task-level quality threshold is met, financial value is evidenced, and risks are within approved limits. Modify if the economics are promising but review costs, routing, or errors are too high. Stop if the workflow cannot beat the manual baseline on quality-adjusted unit economics after a fair test. A failed pilot is a successful decision if it prevents an expensive rollout.

Here is the complete formula. Realized financial value minus all incremental operating costs equals net benefit. Net benefit divided by incremental operating costs equals operating ROI. Track the one-time implementation costs separately and calculate payback before scaling. Keep capacity gains as a separate operational measure until their business value is verified.

For our hypothetical workflow, sixty-seven hours saved was the starting point. The decisive number was seventy-two point five percent: the proportion of potential value that must be realized to break even on recurring cost. A dashboard that omits that distinction is measuring activity, not investment performance.

If you are evaluating an AI agent, ask one question before the next budget meeting: where, exactly, will the freed capacity produce measurable value?
